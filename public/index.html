Fact check · Governor's announcement of Nov. 17, 2026

Michigan's school pension isn't fully funded yet. It might be by 2030.

The Governor announced that MPSERS, the pension for more than 400,000 current and retired school employees, is “Fully Funded Ahead of Schedule.” We checked each claim against the state's own reports. Most of the numbers hold up. The headline doesn't, and the 2030 date is a forecast the state's consultant first made (as 2031) in January 2025.

Claim by claim

What holds up, and what doesn't

Quotes are from the Governor's release. Findings come from MPSERS's actuarial valuations, its annual report, and the State of Michigan Investment Board's own materials.

Michigan Public School Pension System Fully Funded Ahead of Schedule (headline)
Not yet. The latest actuarial valuation puts the pension at 74.6% funded, $25.1 billion short, as of Sept. 30, 2025 (79.6% if assets are counted at market value instead of the smoothed value the actuaries use). The release's own text says “on track to reach full funding by 2030.”
✕Misleading
on track to reach full funding by 2030, rather than its previously projected date under current law of 2038
2038 is the deadline in law for paying off the debt, not a forecast. In January 2025, Treasury's investment consultant (Aon) already projected full funding by fiscal 2031 in its central case. Two strong years (9.8% in FY2025, 14% reported for FY2026) can plausibly pull that to 2030, but only if markets cooperate. See the forecast range.
~Plausible forecast
reduce future pension costs by approximately $3.2 billion annually, with about $1.5B from the state and $1.7B from districts
The figure matches the yearly debt payment the law locks in. The actuaries computed $3.19 billion for FY2025, and a 2017–18 “floor” keeps the payment from falling below the prior year's until the debt is paid off. It stops at payoff. The roughly $0.8 billion a year for benefits being earned now continues. How the payment works.
✓Checks out, if 2030 happens
In Fiscal Year 2026, the state earned a 14% return … a 10-year annualized return of 10.7%, exceeding the 9.5% median
Consistent with the Investment Board's latest published numbers: as of Dec. 31, 2025, a 10-year return of 10.1% against a peer median of 9.0%. The FY2026 results aren't published yet; they go to the Board on Dec. 16. The fund also trailed its own benchmark in every period shown. Returns detail.
~Consistent, not yet published
years of disciplined pension contributions … above what is called for in the current statute
The state did make extra one-time deposits: $1.14 billion in FY2023 and $250 million in FY2025. But the main force is the payment floor written into laws passed in 2017 and 2018, before this administration. A 2024 law (PA 127) also cut districts' share and ended retiree-health contribution floors; critics called the related budget shift a “raid.”
✓True, with context
MPSERS' retiree healthcare benefits reached fully funded status in 2023
True. The retiree-health plan was 150.6% funded as of Sept. 30, 2025. (The $598 million savings figure in the release was not checked here.)

How funded is it?

Up from about 60% to 74.6% in ten years

“Funded” means assets divided by the benefits already earned. The official figure uses a five-year smoothed asset value, so good and bad years phase in gradually. On Sept. 30, 2025, smoothed assets were 94% of market value, so gains are still waiting to be counted.

Where it might be now

The official count for Sept. 30, 2026 won't come until the next valuation (last year's was issued March 3). We made a rough estimate from published figures: about 88% at market value. Closing the remaining gap of about $11 billion by 2030 needs returns somewhat above the plan's 6% assumption, with the debt payment held near $3.2 billion a year.

Our back-of-envelope (not an official figure)
  • Start: market-value assets of $78.66B (MPSERS ACFR, Sept. 30, 2025) and liabilities of $98.80B (2025 valuation).
  • FY2026: assets earn the release's 14%; cash flow repeats FY2025's (contributions $4.90B in, benefits and expenses $5.83B out). Liabilities grow at the 6% assumption, plus normal cost (13.79% of $9.80B payroll), minus benefits.
  • Result: about $88.7B against $100.1B, or about 88.6%, with a gap of about $11.4B. The real figure will differ: MPSERS's own FY2026 return and cash flows aren't published yet.
  • Rolled forward with the debt payment at $3.19B a year: at 6% returns, 100% arrives in about FY2031; at 7.5% a year, about FY2029; at 4% a year, not by 2035.

The $3.2 billion

Why it's paying off early: a floor on the debt payment

Each year the actuaries compute a payment toward the unfunded liability. Strong returns have pushed that number down. But laws passed in 2017 and 2018 (PA 92 of 2017, PA 181 of 2018) say the payment can't fall below the prior year's, in dollars, “until such time as the UAAL is ‘paid off.’” PA 127 of 2024 reset that floor to the FY2025 amount. Paying more than the formula asks is what pulls the payoff date forward.

What stops at full funding

The debt payment, about $3.2 billion a year. The release splits it as about $1.5B from the state (through School Aid Section 147c) and about $1.7B from districts. Since FY2026, a district's debt-payment rate is capped at 15.21% of payroll, down from 20.96% (PA 127 of 2024); the state covers the rest.

What keeps going

The normal cost, the price of benefits current staff earn each year: $0.83 billion for FY2028 (8.45% of pension payroll). The retirement board's funding policy keeps it in place until the plan is 120% funded. Whether districts or the state keep the freed-up debt money is a budget decision lawmakers haven't made.

Investment returns

Ahead of peers, behind its own benchmark

The release credits “strong investment stewardship.” Against other large public pensions, the fund has done well. Against the benchmark its own board sets (a blend of indexes matching its target mix), it trailed in every period. Much of the gain came from strong markets.

What the fund owns

A pooled $122 billion, nearly half in private assets

MPSERS doesn't hold securities in its own name. Its money (about $94 billion with retiree health) is pooled with the state employees', state police and judges' systems and managed by Treasury's Bureau of Investments. Three public sources show what's inside.

Other places to look

The MPSERS annual report (Investment Section) lists MPSERS's top 10 stocks and top 10 bonds. On Sept. 30, 2025 the stocks were led by Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Amazon and Meta, and the bonds were mostly U.S. Treasuries. It says “a complete list of stock and bond holdings is available from the Michigan Department of Treasury,” so the full list is available on request. The Investment Board's quarterly reports break down each asset class, including private-equity and real-estate managers.

The timing

Announced two weeks after the election, ahead of the official numbers

The release didn't reveal anything that was being held back: the early-payoff forecast had been public since January 2025. But it arrived before the Investment Board has reviewed the year's results and months before the actuaries measure the plan again. It also makes a policy request of whoever governs next.

The request in the release

“Future gubernatorial administrations and legislatures will need to work together to ensure the pension system reaches full funding but is not overfunded. Overpayments into the trust will result in a loss of dollars that could be used in the classroom.”Governor's release, Nov. 17, 2026

The case for stopping at 100%

If payments simply continue, Treasury's own study projected a median funded ratio of 154% by 2043. Money beyond what's needed sits in the trust instead of classrooms.

The case for a cushion

At exactly 100%, one bad market year puts the plan back under water. The retirement board's own policy keeps normal-cost payments in place until the plan is 120% funded, and Treasury's study put a bad-markets case (1 in 20) at 59% in 2033.

Closer to home: Troy School District

For Troy, about $15 million a year, if the state lets districts keep it

Every district pays a charge toward the MPSERS debt. Since October 2025 it has been 15.21% of payroll. When the debt is paid off, that charge ends. At Troy's current payroll, that's about $14.8 million a year, if the state passes it through instead of offsetting it, which it hasn't always done.

How big $14.8M is for Troy

  • 7.3% of 2025–26 general fund spending ($202.2M).
  • About 2.4–2.5 times the deficits in Troy's February 2026 four-year forecast.
  • About 1.2 times the board's “$12 million in reductions” target (March 2026).
  • Cross-check: Troy's share of MPSERS (0.85944%) × the release's $1.7B district share = $14.6M.

When it would arrive

Not before the 2030–31 school year, and only if “2030” means the last debt payment. If it means a valuation date, the relief could come around 2032–33, because valuations set rates about three years out. Troy's four-year forecast ends in 2028–29 and has no MPSERS line, so this money doesn't help the deficits the board is cutting for now.

The last two rounds of “relief”

Troy's own documents show why the $14.8M isn't guaranteed. The state has reshaped MPSERS savings before:

  • 2024–25: the $598 million the state redirected to schools reached Troy as a one-time payment of $5,055,278. Troy's administration called it “Essentially one-time money” (budget presentation, Jan. 20, 2026). Troy's audited report said the state gave it “rather than a traditional increase to the foundation allowance.”
  • 2025–26: the debt charge dropped from 20.96% to 15.21% of payroll. That one-time payment ended, and other state offsets changed. Troy raised its retirement budget by $3,612,635 for “unanticipated increases in employer contribution rates.” Its January 2026 planning deck put it this way: “Retirement and health-care costs once covered by State have been shifted to the employer.”
  • Net effect (our calculation): after subtracting the state's MPSERS offsets, Troy's own MPSERS cost was 27.24% of payroll in 2024–25 and 27.27% in 2025–26, essentially unchanged. One 2024–25 offset (Section 147a(1)) wasn't found and isn't counted.

Sources: Troy School District FY2023–FY2026 audits and Plante Moran management letters; budget presentations of Jan. 13 and Jan. 20, 2026; “TSD – 4-Year General Fund Forecast, February 2026” (Mar. 7, 2026 retreat); Budget Amendment 2 (Mar. 17, 2026). All are in the public Troy board document archive. No Troy document mentions 2038, the payoff date, or pension full funding.

What to ask

Questions the release leaves open

  1. What assumptions produce 2030: which returns, what contributions, and on the smoothed or market-value basis?
  2. Does “2030” mean the last debt payment, or the valuation date that shows full funding? Valuations set rates about three years out, so the answer moves the savings by up to three years.
  3. Is the $3.2 billion the floored debt payment, and how exactly is it split between the state and districts?
  4. Will the board's 120% funding target stay in place, or does “not overfunded” mean stopping at 100%?
  5. When the debt payment ends, does the districts' $1.7 billion stay with districts, or will the state recapture it through school aid?
  6. Will Treasury publish the complete holdings list its annual report says is available?
